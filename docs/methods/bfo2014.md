# Transport boundary scheme (Benamou-Froese-Oberman 2014)

The second boundary value problem asks for a convex `u` with `det D^2 u = f` in a domain `X` whose gradient maps `X` onto a convex target `Y`: the optimal transport map, for the quadratic cost, from the density `f` on `X` to the uniform density on `Y` (`Transport(target)`, T12 and T16 in the catalogue).
Benamou, Froese and Oberman write the condition on the boundary as a Hamilton-Jacobi equation, discretise it by monotone upwind differences, and solve it together with a monotone or filtered scheme for the equation inside, by Newton's method.
maesolvers ships it as the scheme `SecondBoundary`, built from the kernels of `Monotone17`, `Filtered` and `Standard9`, solved by `Newton`, and as the preset `FDTransport` (ADR 0004).
Reproduction: [`benchmarks/bfo2014.md`](../../benchmarks/bfo2014.md).

```python
from maesolvers import T16, FDTransport, Newton, SecondBoundary

result = FDTransport(n=128).solve(T16)  # the preset
same = Newton(
    scheme=SecondBoundary(n=128),
    start=Newton(scheme=SecondBoundary(n=128, interior="monotone"), max_iter=200),
    max_iter=200,
).solve(T16)
```

Source: J.-D. Benamou, B. D. Froese, A. M. Oberman, "Numerical solution of the optimal transportation problem using the Monge-Ampere equation", J. Comput. Phys. 260 (2014) 107-126, doi 10.1016/j.jcp.2013.12.015, Sections 2 to 5; "the paper" below.
The convergence proof is in the companion paper (their reference [4], arXiv 1208.4873).

## The boundary condition

With `H` the signed distance to the boundary of `Y` (positive outside), the condition `grad u(X) = Y` holds when `H(grad u(x)) = 0` on the boundary of `X`.
For a convex `Y` and a convex `u`, the paper's Lemma 2.1 writes it through the support function `H*(n) = sup_{y in Y} y . n` of the target:

```text
H(grad u(x)) = sup over |n| = 1 with n . n_x > 0 of ( grad u(x) . n - H*(n) ),
```

with `n_x` the outward normal of `X` at `x`.
On a grid over a square, at a node of the boundary ring the supremum runs over finitely many directions (`admissible`):

- on a side, the directions with `n . n_x > 0`, the open half-plane;
- at a corner, the closed quadrant between the normals of its two faces (the paper's "formal limits of the obliqueness constraint").

The directions are the four face normals for a `Box` target, where the boundary condition is exact (the paper, Section 6.1: "there is no need to discretise its boundary"; on a side it is then a Neumann condition), and otherwise `N_Y` directions `n_j = (cos 2 pi j / N_Y, sin 2 pi j / N_Y)` (`uniform_directions`, the paper's Section 2.2; `directions`, 512 when None), which replace the target by a polygon with an `O(1 / N_Y)` error.
`grad u . n` is the upwind difference of the paper's Section 4.3.1 (`upwind_gradient`),

```text
grad u . n ~ max(n_1, 0) D_1^- u + min(n_1, 0) D_1^+ u + max(n_2, 0) D_2^- u + min(n_2, 0) D_2^+ u,
```

which reaches into the square only, for every admissible direction, and is monotone; it is first order in `h`, which limits the gradient of the solution to first order (the paper, Section 5.2.2).
The boundary equation at a ring node is the largest `upwind grad u . n - H*(n)` over its admissible directions, and its Jacobian row is the upwind difference of the direction that attains it (Danskin's theorem).

## The equation inside

Every node of the grid of `n` cells per side over the square carries an unknown, `(n + 1)^2` in all.
At an interior node the equation is

```text
MA[u] = f + u(x_0),
```

with `x_0` the node nearest the centre of the square: `u(x_0)` in the right-hand side picks one solution of a problem whose solutions differ by a constant, and makes the Jacobian invertible (the paper, Sections 3 and 4.3.2).
At the discrete solution `u(x_0)` is of the order of the discretisation error, not zero (`Result.info["u_anchor"]`).
`MA` is (`interior`):

- `"monotone"`: the operator of `Monotone17` (`docs/methods/monotone17.md`), the smallest `a^+ b^+` over the orthogonal pairs of the stencil, continued below zero with the slope `kappa`, with the pairs whose arms leave the square left out (`boundary="narrow"`).
  The paper's compact scheme is the 9-point stencil (`stencil=9`, the default), with `max(a, delta) max(b, delta) - min(a, delta) - min(b, delta)` and `delta = h^2` in place of `Monotone17`'s continuation; both are monotone and differ by `O(h^2)` where `u` is convex.
- `"filtered"` (the default, the paper's computations): `MA_M + eps S((MA_A - MA_M) / eps)`, the filter of Froese-Oberman 2013 (`docs/methods/filtered.md`) between the monotone operator `MA_M` and the standard 9-point one `MA_A = u_xx u_yy - u_xy^2`, with the paper's width `eps = sqrt(h) + dtheta` (Section 5.2.1), `dtheta = pi/4` for the compact stencil; the Jacobian is Froese and Oberman's `(1 - S') J_M + max(S', 0) J_A`.
  This width is about ten times `Filtered`'s default `sqrt(h) + dtheta / 10`, so the standard scheme holds almost everywhere on a smooth solution.

The target density is uniform, so the right-hand side `F(x, grad u) = f(x) / rho_Y(grad u)` of the paper is `f(x)` and its derivative in `grad u` vanishes.

## Domains that are not the square

The grid covers a square: `box`, or the domain's bounding box when `box` is None, which must then be a square.
A domain inside it is embedded as in the paper's Section 2.3: `f` is zero at the nodes outside the closed domain, so the gradient maps the rest of the square onto the boundary of the target.
The result reports the nodes, values and centred Hessian at the grid nodes in the closed domain only, the points where the problem is posed, so `evaluate` and the gradient-map error are taken there; `Result.u` is the spline through every node of the square.

## The start and the iteration

`solve_laplace` is the paper's start (Section 5.3): the Poisson equation `laplace u = k rhs` with the 5-point Laplacian inside and the Neumann data `grad u . n_x = y_0(x) . n_x` of the affine map `y_0` of the square onto the target's bounding box on the ring (one-sided, along the diagonal at a corner), the constant `k` that makes the discrete Neumann problem solvable, and `u(x_0) = 0`.
`Newton` calls it with `rhs = 2 sqrt(f)`.

`FDTransport` is Newton on the filtered scheme from the root of the monotone scheme on the same grid (`start`, at most 200 steps), the pattern of `FDHybrid`.
On T16, a smooth problem on the square itself, Newton converges from the paper's Poisson start too, in 3 steps at every grid, as the paper's 2 or 3; the preset then takes 3 or 2 steps after 4 or 5 on the start, the same root at a higher cost.
The preset keeps the monotone start because on an embedded domain the Poisson start leaves Newton far from any root (gradient errors 0.27 to 0.98 on the paper's ellipse).
On a domain embedded in the square (T12, the ellipse of the paper's Table 2, the half-disks of its Table 3), Newton on the filtered scheme makes no progress from the Poisson start; from the monotone root it converges at `N = 32` and stalls from `N = 64` (T12, the half-disks) or 128 (the ellipse) on, at nodes outside the domain where the filter sits at its kink (`benchmarks/bfo2014.md`, Finding 2).
The full Newton step of the paper diverges there; the stall is the one of `FDFiltered` (#56), and the damped step is kept until that issue settles the solve.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Default | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `n` | 64 | cells per side of the square, `h = side / n` (ADR 0001) |
| `interior` | `"filtered"` | `"filtered"` or `"monotone"` (scheme only; the preset is filtered) |
| `stencil` | 9 | points of the monotone stencil: 9 (the paper's), 17 or 33 |
| `epsilon` | None | filter width; None is `sqrt(h) + dtheta`, `dtheta` twice `angular_resolution(stencil)` |
| `directions` | None | `N_Y`, at least 4; None is the face normals for a `Box` target, 512 otherwise |
| `box` | None | the square of the grid; None is the domain's bounding box |
| `jacobian_floor` | `1e-12` | floor of the monotone Jacobian's factors, times `1 / (2 h^2)` |
| `spline_degree` | 3 | degree of the spline through the node values |
| `max_iter`, `tol` | 200, `1e-10` | Newton on the filtered scheme (preset); the start keeps 200 steps |

## A target of your own

The scheme reads the target only through `support_function(target, n)`.
It knows `Box`, `Disk` and `Ellipse`; a target of another type, such as the rotated ellipse of the paper's Table 2, has the method `support_function(n)` of the protocol `SupportFunction` (`docs/guide.md`, "Transport problems").
`FDTransport.check` also runs `check_mass_balance`, which needs `volume(target)`; for such a target, compose `Newton(scheme=preset.scheme, start=preset.start)` and check the balance yourself.

## Known limits

- 2D only, on a square grid; the target density is uniform, so the paper's Tables 4 to 6 (Gaussian and Dirac target densities) are out of reach of `Transport(target)`.
- The upwind boundary condition is first order, so the gradient map converges at first order (second order in `u` on smooth problems).
- On a domain embedded in the square, Newton on the filtered scheme stalls from `N = 64` or 128 (above); the result is reported with `converged` False, and its error stops falling at about 0.06 on the paper's ellipse and 0.019 on its half-disks, where the paper's keeps halving (`benchmarks/bfo2014.md`, Findings 2 and 3: a mismatch for Tables 2 and 3).
- The paper's Table 1 counts `N` nodes per side: `FDTransport(n=N - 1)` prints its max column exactly (`benchmarks/bfo2014.md`, Finding 1).
- The monotone start itself stalls on T12 at `n = 361` and 512 (a `RuntimeWarning`), so there the filtered scheme starts from an iterate that is not a root.
- The monotone interior alone converges slowly on embedded domains, where `f` is zero on part of the square: its angular resolution limits it there (`benchmarks/bfo2014.md`).
- `FDTransport` needs a target whose volume `volume` knows, for its mass-balance check.
